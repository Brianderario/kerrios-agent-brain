# Standard & Works Newsletter Writing Playbook (Lead + whole issue)

scope: agent-prompt-reference · created 2026-06-18 · source: copywork study of five best-in-lane writers (Matt Levine, Byrne Hobart, Doomberg, Brian Potter/Construction Physics, Packy McCormick), commissioned after Brian's note that S&W Leads read choppy and middle-of-the-road and that Lead quality is the newsletter's growth lever.

The writer agent loads this every run and writes the Lead to it. The editor agent checks the draft Lead against it and rewrites the Lead if it falls short. This is the standard that produced the 2026-06-18 "Washington Trades Grants for Equity" Lead, which Brian rated "10x better than anything produced so far."

## The one rule above all
Do NOT open the Lead on the news. Open on a HOOK. The news event is the test case for an idea you state first; it is never the first sentence. This single move is the difference between the Lead Brian rejected and the one he approved.

## Hook patterns (pick one)
- **Inversion (Doomberg):** state the old reality, then the turn. "Washington spent two years paying companies to build chips. This week it started buying a piece of them instead." Two sentences, one idea, creates a loop.
- **Rule-first, then bend it (Matt Levine):** state in plain words how the world normally works, then show this week's event breaking the rule. CAUTION: this move tips into AI slop fast (see anti-slop note). Keep it to one clean sentence; do not build it into a listed triad capped by a cute one-liner.
- **Naive question / itch (Byrne Hobart):** voice the question the reader has had but never resolved. Number in sentence one, the itch in sentence two.
- **Puzzle / reversal (Brian Potter):** "Everyone assumes X. The evidence says otherwise." Make the topic strange before explaining it.
- **Fact + promise (Packy McCormick):** the news, then immediately what you'll do with it. Loop closed only by reading on.

## The engine of the second paragraph
- **Isomorphism (Hobart) — the S&W signature, compressed:** take the specific datapoint, name the structural force it is an instance of, state what that force predicts. ("The fabs are sold out, so the next dollar flows to what feeds them.") This IS the "draw a line between data points" rule.
- **Ownership / incentive translation (Levine):** restate the official rationale in blunt, self-interested terms. The gap between what is said and what is meant is the takeaway. Never write "the key takeaway is."
- **Inverted so-what (Hobart/Doomberg):** end on what the number actually means, not what it says. Hand the reader a reusable lens, not a fact.

## Numbers
- Never naked. One hero number, framed by relation: a yardstick (Potter: "as much water as a town of 50,000"), a before/after delta (McCormick: "from $100M to $500 in 21 years"), or a second-order implication (Hobart: the $ that can't be spent without destroying its own value).
- One number, not five (Levine). Pick the one that proves the thesis; cut the rest.
- Plain idea first, technical term second, one-clause gloss inline (Potter). Keep the real word; attach a handle. ("indium phosphide, the crystal behind the optical parts that move data between chips.")

## Rhythm and voice
- Long-build sentence, then a short verdict sentence. The short one carries the judgment and the emphasis (Levine, McCormick).
- Declarative, subject-verb-object, strong physical verbs, no hedging-by-adjective (Doomberg). Calibrated qualifiers ("almost certainly," "the base case") only where the claim is genuinely uncertain.
- Confidence comes from the exact number and the mechanism, not from intensifiers.

## Anti-slop note (Brian, 2026-06-18)
Even a "good" technique becomes slop when it gets too constructed. Brian cut this line from an otherwise-approved Lead:
> "For two years CHIPS worked like a grant program: Washington wrote the check, the company kept the company, and the public got jobs and a factory. A stake rewrites that."
Why it is slop: a manufactured rule-of-three list ("wrote the check, kept the company, got jobs and a factory") followed by a cute setup-payoff one-liner ("A stake rewrites that"). It performs structure instead of carrying a fact. If the hook already established the shift, do not restate it as a tidy triad-plus-punchline. Cut straight to the substance ("The government now sits on the cap table of the firms it backs...").

## Leave at the door (tone-specific, wrong for a capex briefing)
- Levine's cartoon mock-dialogue, the puzzlement "?" tic, footnote sprawl, self-deprecation.
- Doomberg's conviction-as-fact on the unknowable, named-villain polemic, cute epigraphs, withheld payoff.
- McCormick's exclamation marks, emoji, memes, hype adjectives, class-clown asides.
- Hobart's length, multiple maxims, grand 100-year-historian frame.
- House hard rules regardless: no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no hype words, and none of the AI-writing tells — especially the "not just X but Y" / "no longer X, it's Y" negation formula, rule-of-three triads, Wikipedia voice, and setup-payoff filler.

## Lead-sized recipe (two paragraphs, ~150-300 words)
1. Hook (1-2 sentences): inversion or rule-first or naive question. No news yet.
2. The deals/event, delivered with one glossed technical handle and the hero number.
3. The turn: name the structural force (isomorphism) and what it predicts.
4. Inverted so-what: what it actually means for a capital allocator, as a lens they can reuse. End there.

## Worked example (approved 2026-06-18)
Hook: "Washington has spent two years paying companies to build chips in America. This week it started buying a piece of them instead." Then the two deals with the indium-phosphide gloss; "Neither deal builds a fab. Both fund what surrounds one." Then straight to the equity substance (cap table, Intel-stake precedent) + the TSMC sold-out-through-2027 isomorphism + close on the government becoming a shareholder in the overlooked layers. (Note: the discarded "grant program... A stake rewrites that" bridge is the anti-slop example above; the approved Lead omits it.)

# PART 2 — THE WHOLE ISSUE (beyond the Lead)

Built 2026-06-18 (build-loop deep-craft pass) from a second research sweep: Axios Smart Brevity, Morning Brew, Stratechery, Lenny, and B2B open/click data, plus a bullet-level re-read of the same five writers studied for the Lead. Part 1 above governs the opening. Part 2 governs the subject line, the sector bullets, the FLOOR/WATCH line, the close, and the voice that ties them together. The bar is the 2026-06-18 issue ("Washington Trades Grants for Equity"), measured against below. The writer applies this when building roundups; the editor checks the draft issue against the rubric.

## Subject line + preview (the open driver)
The subject names the single most consequential thing in the issue, stated plainly. The preview text is a second subject, not a repeat: it adds the next hook or the stakes. The 6/18 subject "Washington Trades Grants for Equity" is the model: the Lead's thesis in four words, a named tension, no clickbait. Patterns:
- **The thesis (best):** the Lead's argument compressed. "Washington Trades Grants for Equity."
- **The named tension:** "The Navy Wants 31 Hulls. Yards Can Build 19." (preview: "The 12-hull gap is the whole maritime trade.")
- **The reprice:** "Copper Is Now a Defense Input." (preview: "The stockpile math changes everything below it.")
- **The turn:** "Memory Prices Turned." (preview: "The bottom is louder than the rebound for hyperscaler capex.")
Front-load the proper noun or number, keep it under ~6 strong words, and let the preview carry the "Plus" list or the second hook (the 6/18 preview did this: "Plus: a $9.7B copper mine clears Argentina, BNSF's $4B desert rail hub..."). Never let the preview repeat the subject.

## The "so what" bullet (the core skill)
A reporting bullet states what happened. A framing bullet states what it changes for the reader's capital. The whole craft is that move. Bake the significance into the same sentence with a consequence clause after the fact: `[bold entity] did [thing], [which moves / which means / leaving / putting / so that] [second-order effect].` The fact is the setup; the clause after the comma is the value. If a bullet ends on the fact, it failed. The test: would a competing allocator already know this, and does the phrasing tell them something they would reprice on.

One fact, one move. Pick exactly one of three:
- **Structural force (Hobart):** when the item rhymes with a trend. "...one more instance of firms paying a margin tax to turn supply-chain risk into a fixed domestic cost."
- **Incentive restatement (Levine):** when a stated rationale hides the real one. "...the expense buys what customers will now pay up for, a supply chain that never touches Asia."
- **Second-order implication:** when the fact forces someone else's hand. "...leaving its three nearest rivals to bid for the same packaging talent in one county."
A bullet that tries all three is an essay. One fact, one move.

## Numbers in bullets (never naked)
Every figure does one of three jobs or gets cut:
- **Yardstick (Potter):** convert the abstract to a felt scale. "$30 million buys roughly one advanced-packaging line, so this is a single-line bet, not a fab."
- **Delta (Packy, de-hyped):** old number beside new. "Capacity climbs up to tenfold; headcount rises by more than 250."
- **Second-order figure (Hobart/Levine):** what the number does to someone's economics.

## The five exemplar moves at bullet length
(Full writer studies are in Part 1; these are the compressed bullet versions.)
- **Levine:** restate the incentive in plain words (what the money is actually doing).
- **Hobart:** name the structural force the item is an instance of.
- **Doomberg:** invert, old reality then the turn, pivot on one strong verb.
- **Potter:** plain idea first, then the technical term with a one-clause gloss plus a yardstick.
- **Packy (de-hyped):** the before/after delta as a clean fact, no adjectives, no emoji.
Leave at the door: Levine's mock-dialogue and footnotes, Doomberg's named-villain polemic, Packy's hype/emoji/exclamation, Hobart's length, Potter's over-explaining (gloss once, move on).

## Section pacing + scannability
- **Order by consequence, not chronology.** The item that reprices the most goes first; the rest descend.
- **Bold the lead noun** of each bullet (the company, the program, the number) so a skimmer reading only the bold words still gets the section's spine.
- **One line per item, two clauses max, link at the end.** A six-item section should clear in under 20 seconds.
- "Brevity is confidence. Length is fear" (Smart Brevity). Cut the second adjective; one does the work.

## The FLOOR / WATCH line
A bare number is noise; a number with a referent is signal. The FLOOR table carries raw levels. The WATCH line carries the one number that broke a trend, named: level, move, and the threshold that matters. The 6/18 WATCH ("The Philadelphia Fed's factory index jumped to 10.3 in June from -0.4 in May, its first clearly positive reading since winter") is the model: level, move, and why the line matters.

## The close
Do not summarize. Leave a thought that compounds, in the issue's voice, one or two sentences, dry and true, ending on the structural read that is still true after today's news fades. Avoid a cheerful kicker. ("Back Tuesday." is a clean operational sign-off; on a heavier issue, precede it with one forward line, e.g. "The order book says rearmament. The labor data says nobody told the shipyards.")

## Voice through-line
Carry one analytical lens across the issue so it reads as one mind, not a feed. If the Lead argued rearmament is supply-constrained, let Defense, Maritime, and Supply Chain each echo that frame. Credible, lightly distinctive, never cute (a capital-allocator audience, not Morning Brew's retail one). Authority comes from precise nouns and clean cuts, not adjectives or jokes.

## THE WHOLE-ISSUE PRE-DELIVERY RUBRIC
The editor/critic pass checks the draft issue against this before staging (in addition to the Lead recipe in Part 1). Ship only when all pass:
1. **Subject** names the day's most consequential thing (the true lead's thesis); preview adds a second hook, never repeats the subject.
2. **Every bullet ends on a "so what,"** not on the bare fact (consequence clause present; one fact, one move).
3. **Every number does a job** (yardstick / delta / second-order); no naked figures.
4. **Each section is consequence-ordered,** lead noun bolded, one line / two clauses per item.
5. **WATCH line** gives level, move, and threshold, named.
6. **The close** lands a dry forward line; it does not summarize.
7. **Voice through-line** present (one lens echoed across sections).
8. **Comprehensive within each category** (all the most important stories of the last couple of days; more bullets not fewer), accuracy + freshness bar unchanged.
9. **House hard rules:** no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no hype adjectives, none of the AI-writing tells (especially the "not just X but Y" / "no longer X, it's Y" negation formula, rule-of-three triads, Wikipedia voice, setup-payoff).
10. **Every link live and every FLOOR/number re-verified at the literal close** before staging (the Story Freshness + FLOOR re-pull gate).

## WORKED BEFORE / AFTER (real 2026-06-18 bullets)
The 6/18 issue is already strong; the upgrade is ending bullets on the so-what instead of a trailing fact.

1. Nokia (Semiconductors).
BEFORE: "**Nokia** is investing $30 million to expand its photonic chip-packaging plant in Pennsylvania, raising capacity up to tenfold and adding more than 250 jobs, backed by the Shapiro administration."
AFTER: "**Nokia** is putting $30 million into its Pennsylvania photonic-packaging plant, raising capacity up to tenfold, a bet that customers will pay a premium for advanced packaging that never routes wafers through Asia."
(Ends on the incentive restatement, not "backed by the Shapiro administration." One fact, one move.)

2. C.H. Robinson (Supply Chain).
BEFORE: "**C.H. Robinson** launched BidBoardX, matching carriers to committed freight across a network of 450,000 carriers and 75,000 customers moving roughly $23 billion a year."
AFTER: "**C.H. Robinson** launched BidBoardX to match carriers to committed freight, pulling the contract-versus-spot decision that sets truckload margins onto its own platform across $23 billion a year in shipments."
(Ends on what it changes, the margin-setting decision it captures, not the scale stats.)

3. Saxum (Supply Chain).
BEFORE: "**Saxum Investment** signed a 50-year Port of Tacoma lease to build a $250 million, 574,000-square-foot cold-storage and food-processing complex."
AFTER: "**Saxum Investment** signed a 50-year Port of Tacoma lease for a $250 million cold-storage complex, a generational bet that West Coast import food keeps moving through fixed dockside capacity rather than around it."
(Ends on the structural read, why a 50-year cold-storage lease is the story.)

Subject patterns: the 6/18 "Washington Trades Grants for Equity" is the model (thesis in four words). Alternatives in the same register, for range: "The Government Is on the Cap Table Now" or "Washington Stops Renting, Starts Owning."
