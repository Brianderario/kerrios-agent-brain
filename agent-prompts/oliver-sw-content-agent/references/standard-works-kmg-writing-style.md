# Standard & Works / KMG Writing Style Deep Dive

scope: Oliver prompt reference
updated: 2026-06-28

This reference gives Oliver a stable writing standard for Standard & Works and Kerri Media Group content. It does not replace the newsletter production rules, voice file, or lead playbook. It distills them into a broader house style that also applies to social copy, website copy, and front-end editorial surfaces.

## Editorial Promise

Standard & Works exists to explain how the U.S. industrial base is being financed, built, constrained, and repriced.

The reader should leave with one of these:

- A company, factory, contract, financing, or policy move they did not know.
- A clearer view of why that move changes capacity, procurement, supply chains, capital formation, power, labor, or regional economics.
- A reusable lens for reading the next industrial story.

Do not write "interesting industrial news." Write what changes.

## Audience

Primary readers:

- Capital allocators and hard-tech investors.
- Industrial operators and manufacturing executives.
- Defense, energy, semiconductor, maritime, and supply-chain decision makers.
- Economic-development and industrial-policy professionals.

They are busy, numerate, and allergic to vague trend copy. They reward precision and a point of view. They do not need every term simplified, but they need every sentence built clearly.

## House Voice

The voice is:

- Analytical, not academic.
- Sober, not flat.
- Specific, not ornate.
- Briefing-style, not press-release style.
- Human, not over-smoothed.

Default sentence shape:

`Actor + verb + number + place/object + consequence.`

Example pattern:

`Aerojet Rocketdyne opened a 51,000-square-foot solid-rocket motor facility in Camden, Arkansas, adding fixed capacity to a state already bidding for more missile work.`

The line works because it has an actor, verb, number, place, object, and a read-through. It does not need "this matters because."

## Lead Mechanics

The Lead is not a summary. It is the issue's argument.

Open on the hook, not the news:

- Old reality -> turn.
- Rule -> exception.
- Naive question -> evidence.
- Puzzle -> mechanism.

Then introduce the news as the test case.

The Lead must carry:

- One clean hook.
- One hero number or concrete fact.
- Named actors.
- A structural force, such as capacity, procurement, financing, power, labor, regional specialization, or supply-chain risk.
- A bounded read-through for capital allocators.

Avoid opening with:

- "Company announced..."
- "This week..."
- "In a move that..."
- "As the industry..."
- "The future of..."

## Bullet Mechanics

A Standard & Works bullet is one sentence with a source link embedded in the action phrase:

`**Company** [action verb phrase](source-url) rest of one-sentence description.`

Every bullet must have:

- A bold lead noun.
- A live source link.
- A number, date, contract value, capacity figure, jobs figure, facility size, output metric, or other concrete magnitude.
- A consequence clause that tells the reader what the fact changes.

A weak bullet ends at the fact.

Better:

`**Saxum Investment** [signed a 50-year Port of Tacoma lease](source-url) for a $250 million cold-storage complex, a generational bet that West Coast import food keeps moving through fixed dockside capacity rather than around it.`

The consequence can be structural, incentive-based, or second-order. Pick one. Do not cram three reads into one bullet.

## Numbers

Numbers are not decoration. Each one must do work:

- Yardstick: size relative to a familiar scale.
- Delta: before/after movement.
- Rank: largest, first, fourth-largest, record, below prior peak.
- Unit economics: cost per unit, cost per site, output per facility.
- Capacity: square feet, jobs, MW, tons, ships, lines, motors, wafers, TEUs.
- Read-through: what the number forces another actor to do.

If the number does not help the reader price the move, cut it or replace it.

## KMG-Wide Copy

Kerri Media Group copy should carry the same concrete spine, but it can be warmer than Standard & Works.

Use this for KMG websites, capability pages, social surfaces, and publication descriptions:

- Lead with the actual product, publication, event, or service.
- State the audience plainly.
- Use specific proof: audience type, issue cadence, topics, event format, or operating artifact.
- Avoid generic agency language: "full-stack", "strategic partner", "unlock growth", "amplify your brand", "end-to-end solutions."
- Do not overexplain the company. Show the work product quickly.

Good KMG copy sounds like an operator explaining a useful media machine. It does not sound like a marketing agency's homepage.

## Social Copy

Social copy should carry one idea cleanly.

Good posts:

- Start with the concrete fact or the hook.
- Include the number.
- Make one point.
- End on the read-through or a precise invitation.

Avoid:

- "Big news."
- "Thread."
- Vague teaser copy.
- Engagement bait.
- Five-hashtag endings.
- Posting a link with no useful standalone substance.

When repurposing a Standard & Works issue:

1. Pick the strongest claim or number.
2. Rewrite it as a standalone thought.
3. Add one sentence of context.
4. Add the consequence.
5. Link only if the post still works without the click.

## Website And Front-End Editorial Surfaces

Content sites should feel like working editorial products, not generic launch pages.

Principles:

- The first screen should show the publication or content product, not a vague hero claim.
- Use actual issue titles, archive cards, lead stories, categories, author names, dates, and examples.
- Keep the density high enough for serious readers to scan quickly.
- Prefer calm, high-contrast editorial layouts over decorative gradients or generic SaaS cards.
- Use strong typography, durable spacing, and clear hierarchy.
- Design for repeat reading: archive, issue detail, topic sections, newsletter signup, author/editorial notes, and source/proof surfaces.

For Standard & Works:

- The surface should read industrial, analytical, and premium.
- Avoid mascots, playful SaaS illustrations, bright startup gradients, and overly rounded marketing cards.
- The visual system should support tables, numbers, charts, deal lists, markets data, and source-linked bullets.

## Banned Patterns

Never use these in Standard & Works copy:

- Em dashes.
- Exclamation marks.
- "Not just X, but Y."
- "No longer X, it is Y."
- "It is not about X, it is about Y."
- "This matters because."
- "The takeaway."
- "The signal."
- "Unlock."
- "Landscape."
- "Inflection point."
- "Strategic imperative."
- "Groundbreaking."
- "Revolutionary."
- "Game-changing."
- Rule-of-three alliteration.
- Rhetorical setup-payoff filler: "The answer lies in..."
- Anonymous "industry observers say."

## Self-Edit Checklist

Before showing or staging a draft:

- Did the lead open on a hook rather than the news?
- Does every selected story fall inside the issue window?
- Does every bullet have a source link and a concrete number?
- Does every number do a job?
- Does each bullet end on a supported consequence?
- Did the issue include a candidate pool and argument audit when it was a production run?
- Did the draft avoid all banned patterns?
- Did any sentence make a claim stronger than the sources support?
- Does the copy sound like one sharp editor, not a feed assembled by committee?
- If staged in Beehiiv, are title, preview, THE FLOOR, thumbnail, feature tag, and unscheduled review-draft status verified?
