## The framework

Buffett's core test, updated for the AI decade: **buy businesses, not tickers.** Each pick had to pass all five:

1. **Will the product still be bought in 2046?** (Durability of demand, not of the stock price.)
2. **Is the moat structural** — regulation, physics, network effects, contracts, trust — rather than just "we're currently the best"? Structural moats survive management mistakes; talent moats don't.
3. **AI test: is the business un-disruptable by AI, or does AI make it *stronger*?** Either the moat is physical/relational (AI can't collect garbage or replace a member's trust in Costco), or the business sits on rails AI traffic must pay to cross (payments, electricity, lithography, foundries).
4. **Pricing power** — can it raise prices with inflation without losing customers?
5. **Does it self-fund?** No dependence on capital markets' mood to keep compounding.

Deliberately excluded: anything where the 20-year case requires predicting *which* AI model/app wins (that's venture risk, not compounding), anything dependent on one product cycle, and anything where AI plausibly deletes the revenue line (pure content/data resellers, undifferentiated ad-funded media, transactional SaaS).

## The portfolio — 11 names, 3 buckets

Suggested shape: roughly equal weights (~9% each), with Berkshire up-weighted (~15%) as ballast and TSMC down-weighted (~5%) for geopolitical tail risk. Dollar-cost average in over 6–12 months rather than buying in one day. Then look at it once a year, not once a week.

### Bucket 1 — Ballast: compounders that don't care about AI (~35%)

**1. Berkshire Hathaway (BRK.B)** — *the "if only one stock" pick.*
A diversified holding company of insurance float, BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, and ~$300B of deployable cash, now run by Greg Abel (CEO since Jan 1, 2026; Buffett stays chairman). The moat is capital-allocation discipline plus permanence — it's structurally built to survive its founder, and the transition is already done. Insurance and rail benefit modestly from AI (underwriting, logistics); nothing about AI threatens them. What would break the thesis: a culture change toward empire-building acquisitions. Watch the annual letter, nothing else.

**2. Costco (COST)** — *trust as a moat.*
The membership model means Costco's customer is the member, not the advertiser or the supplier — renewal rates ~90%+ and periodic fee increases are the pricing power. AI improves its supply chain and can't replicate the core asset: members' belief that Costco has already done the price-hunting for them. In an AI-generated-everything world, a brand whose entire promise is "we curated honestly" gets *more* valuable. Known caveat: it perpetually looks expensive (~50x forward earnings); over 20 years the business quality has historically bailed out the entry multiple, but expect long flat stretches. If the multiple genuinely scares you, half-size it and add on drawdowns.

**3. Waste Management (WM)** — *the most AI-proof business in America.*
Garbage volume tracks population and consumption, both up over 20 years. The moat is landfill permits — effectively impossible to replicate (nobody zones new landfills), giving pricing power that has outrun inflation for decades. AI/automation is purely accretive here: route optimization and automated sorting lower costs while the revenue line is untouchable by software. Boring is the point.

### Bucket 2 — Tollbooths AI traffic must pay to cross (~30%)

**4. Visa (V)** — *the rails of agentic commerce.*
The bear case (AI agents route around 2–3% interchange via stablecoins) is real enough that Visa is answering it in public: Intelligent Commerce for authenticating AI-agent payments, a stablecoin settlement pilot already at a ~$7B annualized run rate (April 2026, +50% QoQ), 130+ stablecoin-linked card programs, and a Trusted Agent Protocol with Cloudflare. The lesson of every previous "Visa killer" (PayPal, crypto, BNPL): the network absorbs the new rail and clips it. Agentic commerce *increases* transaction count — machines shop more often than humans, and every one of those transactions needs authentication, authorization, and dispute resolution. That's the product. What would break it: regulation forcibly capping interchange globally, or stablecoins winning consumer trust *and* fraud protection — watch, don't predict. (Mastercard is interchangeable here; own either or split.)

**5. Moody's (MCO)** — *a government-licensed duopoly.*
Ratings are a de facto regulatory license (only 10 NRSROs exist; MCO + SPGI hold ~80% of rated issuance) and the cost of a rating is trivial relative to the interest it saves an issuer — durable pricing power. Debt will exist in 2046, and a large 2026–2028 refinancing wall is a near-term tailwind. The AI risk is honest: LLMs erode the *data analytics* side (why pay for a terminal when a model reads the PDFs?). Moody's is the better-protected of the pair because its models are embedded in regulation and its liability-bearing, human-accountable verdicts are exactly what an LLM can't legally supply. Buffett has held it for 25 years for these reasons.

**6. Chubb (CB)** — *AI widens the best underwriter's lead.*
Insurance is the canonical Buffett business: paid today for a promise tomorrow, invest the float in between. Chubb is the disciplined large-cap underwriter (commercial + high-net-worth lines), with ~6 years of algorithmic AI already deployed in underwriting and claims, pushing its expense ratio toward 24–25%. AI in insurance is an amplifier: better risk selection compounds into better float economics, and the risks being insured (cyber, AI liability itself) are *growing*. Chosen over Progressive deliberately: autonomous vehicles are a genuine 20-year headwind for personal auto, while commercial/specialty lines have no equivalent threat. (Berkshire already gives you insurance too; that's fine — it's the best sector for this thesis.)

### Bucket 3 — AI accelerants with physical moats (~35%)

The rule for this bucket: **never own the model, own the physics.** Whichever lab/app wins, they all buy the same chips, machines, electrons, and molecules.

**7. Microsoft (MSFT)** — *distribution beats invention.*
The moat isn't Copilot's quality — it's that Microsoft owns the pipes into essentially every enterprise on earth (80%+ of the Fortune 500 already on its AI services, 20M+ paid Copilot seats, Azure growing ~31%). Whoever builds the best model, Microsoft is the default way corporations *deploy* it. The honest caveat is capex: ~$190B planned for 2026 is a bet that AI demand keeps absorbing supply; ROI on that spend is the number to check once a year. It's the only Big Tech name here because it's the one whose moat is distribution and switching costs rather than a consumer surface AI could re-intermediate.

**8. TSMC (TSM)** — *everyone's foundry. Size it small.*
~90% of leading-edge chips; every AI winner — Nvidia, Apple, AMD, Google's TPUs, Amazon's chips — is its customer, so you're long the race, not a horse. 2026: revenue guided ~+30%, capex raised to $52–56B, demand exceeding capacity. The physics moat (decades of process learning) is near-unassailable commercially. The reason for the ~5% weight instead of 9%: Taiwan. A cross-strait conflict is a low-probability, thesis-ending event that no amount of Arizona/Japan fab diversification fully hedges on a 20-year clock. Own it because the payoff asymmetry justifies a small slot — not more.

**9. ASML (ASML)** — *a literal monopoly, by physics.*
The world's only maker of EUV lithography machines — no advanced chip on earth gets made without them, and the moat is 40 years of accumulated optics/engineering plus a supplier network no competitor (or country, so far) has replicated. 2026 sales guided to €36–40B and rising with every fab TSMC, Samsung, and Intel build. It's the safest way to own semiconductors: cyclical on a 2-year view, monopoly on a 20-year view. Risk: a genuine post-silicon computing paradigm — decades away if ever.

**10. NextEra Energy (NEE)** — *AI's ultimate derivative is the electron.*
Data centers are driving the first real U.S. electricity demand growth in 20 years (~50% of demand growth through 2030; utilities spending a record ~$240B in 2026). NextEra is the largest renewables developer with a ~33 GW backlog, and its announced $67B acquisition of Dominion would bolt on Northern Virginia's "data center alley," the densest AI load on the planet. Regulated returns + contracted renewables = the toll on every token generated. Risks: interest rates (it trades like a bond proxy) and deal-execution/regulatory approval on Dominion. Every scenario where AI matters requires more electricity; that's the whole thesis.

**11. Linde (LIN)** — *molecules on 20-year contracts.*
Industrial gas is a 3-player global oligopoly with on-site plants wired into customers under long take-or-pay contracts — switching is nearly impossible and pricing has outrun inflation for 30 years. The AI kicker: chip fabs are enormous consumers of ultra-pure electronics gases, and Linde's ~$10B backlog is tilting toward semiconductor projects; EPS growth guided to accelerate to 9–10% in 2026. Plus hydrogen optionality if that market ever matures. It's the quietest compounder on this list — precisely why it's here.

## What was left out, and why (so future-you doesn't wonder)

- **Alphabet (GOOGL):** genuinely cheap and owns DeepMind/Waymo/YouTube, but the core search-ad franchise is the *one* mega-cap cash cow where AI disruption is a live, unresolved question. A 20-year sleep-well portfolio shouldn't carry the market's single biggest disruption debate. If you want it, it's an opportunistic add, not a core hold.
- **Nvidia:** spectacular business, but the pick violates rule 3-of-the-bucket — its margins *are* the prize competitors (and its own customers) are attacking. TSMC + ASML capture the same wave with structurally safer moats.
- **Pure-play pharma:** patent cliffs mean the business must reinvent itself every 12 years; that's a bet on pipelines, not moats.
- **Banks:** leveraged, commoditized, and periodically self-immolating; Berkshire gives curated financial exposure instead.
- **Amazon, Union Pacific, Sherwin-Williams, Brookfield, Mastercard, S&P Global, Progressive:** all pass most tests — fine substitutes if any core name becomes un-ownable. Kept off only to avoid redundancy with names already in the list.

## Operating instructions (the part that matters more than the picks)

1. **DCA in over 6–12 months.** No lump-sum timing heroics.
2. **Look once a year.** The only annual questions: Is the moat intact? Did management do something dumb with capital? Everything else — quarters, multiples, macro — is noise on a 20-year clock.
3. **Sell triggers are thesis breaks, not price drops:** Visa losing the agentic-payment layer, Costco monetizing members' trust, Berkshire empire-building, Taiwan conflict (TSM), Microsoft's capex ROI never showing up. A 40% drawdown with the moat intact is a buying opportunity, not a signal.
4. **Rebalance with new money, not by selling winners** (taxes + the winners are usually winning for a reason).
5. This list should be roughly as boring in 2036 as it is today. If it feels exciting, something's wrong.
